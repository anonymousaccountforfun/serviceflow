# 1. Executive assessment

## 1.1 What ProService appears to be

ProService Hawaii is a Hawaii-centered **professional employer organization**, or PEO. Its core proposition is to let small and midsize employers outsource or co-employ significant portions of:

- payroll and payroll-tax administration;
- employee-benefits access and administration;
- workers’ compensation;
- human-resources advice and compliance;
- safety and risk management;
- recruiting, onboarding, and employee lifecycle administration;
- timekeeping and related workforce systems; and
- training and management support.

Public descriptions consistently position ProService as Hawaii’s largest or leading local HR-outsourcing provider. Its differentiation appears to rest less on unique payroll software than on a bundled combination of:

1. local regulatory and labor-market knowledge;
2. pooled benefits and insurance purchasing;
3. human service;
4. compliance and risk-management infrastructure;
5. a dense local customer base; and
6. integration of many employer workflows through one commercial relationship.

That bundle is economically important. A PEO can appear externally to be a payroll processor, but its revenue, working-capital, insurance, fiduciary, and compliance profile is materially more complex.

## 1.2 Publicly reported scale

Public-facing ProService materials have variously used figures around:

| Metric | Publicly stated scale | Definition risk |
|---|---:|---|
| Client companies | approximately **3,000** | May mean active PEO clients, locations, accounts, or all customers |
| Worksite employees | approximately **50,000** | May mean covered, paid, active, or enrolled employees |
| Internal ProService staff | approximately **400** | May include mainland staff, acquired companies, contractors, or ProCare |
| Hawaii private-sector footprint | potentially about **1 in 10** private-sector employees | Modeled from the reported 50,000 WSE figure; not a company-reported market share |
| Implied WSEs per client | approximately **16.7** | Simple model: 50,000 ÷ 3,000 |
| Implied internal staff per client | approximately **0.13** | Simple model: 400 ÷ 3,000 |
| Implied WSEs per internal employee | approximately **125** | Simple model: 50,000 ÷ 400 |

These are useful order-of-magnitude indicators, **not audited operating statistics**. The exact numerator, denominator, date, and perimeter must be obtained internally.

The modeled ratios suggest a company serving a highly fragmented SMB base, with significant human-service requirements. An average near 17 WSEs per client would imply that many clients are smaller than conventional national middle-market PEO accounts. That is strategically attractive because small clients can have high retention and value local expertise, but it also raises onboarding, service-cost, account-minimum, and unit-economics questions.

## 1.3 Ownership and strategic context

The public ownership arc appears to include:

1. creation and local growth beginning in the mid-1990s;
2. expansion under long-serving leadership;
3. institutional investment by **FFL Partners**;
4. a subsequent investment or acquisition involving **Silver Lake**; and
5. continued operation under the ProService brand rather than absorption into a national payroll brand.

Silver Lake’s involvement is a strong signal that the investment thesis likely extends beyond a stable local-services annuity. Plausible thesis components include:

- digitizing a locally dominant PEO;
- improving service leverage and operating margins;
- adding products to the installed base;
- using AI and workflow automation to lower cost-to-serve;
- executing acquisitions;
- expanding beyond Hawaii through adjacent geographies or specialized offerings;
- separating non-core assets such as ProCare; and
- building a repeatable platform attractive to another sponsor or strategic buyer.

Those are **inferences**, not disclosed board strategy.

## 1.4 Board-level conclusion

ProService appears to own a rare local franchise: a large portion of Hawaii’s private-employer base entrusts it with payroll, benefits, workers’ compensation, compliance, and employee data. This should create:

- recurring and embedded revenue;
- high switching costs;
- privileged employer and workforce data;
- distribution leverage;
- purchasing scale;
- local brand authority; and
- multiple opportunities for product expansion.

However, the same model concentrates risk. Potential exposures include:

- a single-state economic base;
- Hawaii tourism and hospitality cyclicality;
- insurance loss development;
- health-benefit renewal shocks;
- payroll-tax and fiduciary errors;
- cyber and privacy incidents;
- customer-funds controls;
- regulatory changes affecting co-employment;
- dependence on a small number of carriers or benefit partners;
- service degradation during automation;
- ambiguity created by acquisitions and brand extensions; and
- a mainland expansion thesis that may underestimate how much of the moat is Hawaii-specific.

A new CEO’s first obligation should therefore be to establish one reconciled fact base across customer counts, WSEs, retention, product economics, insurance risk, technology progress, service quality, and legal entities before accelerating growth.

# 2. Company history and ownership timeline

## 2.1 Founding and early development

ProService traces its founding to **1994**. Public company histories describe a business created to provide Hawaii employers with outsourced HR administration.

The date is strategically relevant because a business operating since 1994 has had time to accumulate:

- durable local referral networks;
- long-tenured employer relationships;
- proprietary Hawaii employment-law knowledge;
- historical workers’ compensation and payroll data;
- operational routines difficult for a new entrant to reproduce;
- carrier and broker relationships; and
- a locally recognized employment brand.

The founding date and original legal entity should be validated against Hawaii business-registration records, IRS PEO records, and historical corporate documents.

Primary company source: [ProService “About Us”](https://www.proservice.com/about-us/).

## 2.2 Ben Godsey era

Ben Godsey is publicly associated with ProService’s development into a large Hawaii PEO. Public biographies have described him as a long-serving CEO and later a chair or board-level leader.

The exact chronology matters. Internal diligence should distinguish:

- date joined;
- date appointed CEO;
- date moved from CEO to chair;
- whether chair status is executive or non-executive;
- current equity ownership;
- rights retained after sponsor transactions;
- role in major customer, carrier, or government relationships; and
- continuing influence over acquisitions and strategy.

The board should not treat “founder,” “CEO,” “chair,” and “owner” as interchangeable labels.

- employ internal staff;
- enter co-employment agreements;
- process payroll;
- hold customer funds;
- sponsor benefit plans;
- procure workers’ compensation;
- operate captive-insurance vehicles;
- hold software intellectual property;
- employ mainland staff;
- own acquired businesses; or
- provide services that are not PEO services.

A board report must therefore distinguish **brand perimeter** from **legal perimeter**.

# 5. Client, WSE, employee, and geography figures

## 5.1 Definitions

A board-grade operating dashboard should use controlled definitions:

- **Client company:** a legal employer with a currently effective contract.
- **Billing account:** a separately invoiced division or location.
- **Worksite employee:** a worker included in the PEO co-employment relationship.
- **Paid WSE:** a WSE receiving wages in the measurement period.
- **Benefit-eligible WSE:** an employee meeting plan-eligibility criteria.
- **Covered life:** an enrolled employee plus covered dependents.
- **Internal employee:** an employee of the ProService operating organization, excluding WSEs.
- **Geographic customer:** measured by employee work location, not only billing address.

## 5.2 Public scale and modeled implications

Using approximately 3,000 clients and 50,000 WSEs:

- average WSEs per client: **16.7**;
- one client equals approximately **0.033%** of the reported client base;
- 100 basis points of client churn would equal approximately **30 clients**;
- 100 basis points of WSE churn would equal approximately **500 WSEs**;
- a $10 monthly increase in retained gross profit per WSE would produce roughly **$6 million annualized gross profit**, before churn and service-cost effects;
- a one-hour annual service-cost reduction per WSE at a loaded internal cost of $40 per hour would be worth approximately **$2 million annually**.

These are modeled illustrations, not company forecasts.

## 5.3 Geographic concentration

The public identity and messaging are overwhelmingly Hawaii-centered. The company has historically emphasized service to Hawaii employers and local expertise.

The board nevertheless needs WSE and gross-profit distributions by:

- Oahu;
- Maui;
- Hawaii Island;
- Kauai;
- Molokai and Lanai;
- Hawaii-based employees working remotely elsewhere;
- mainland customers;
- mainland employees of Hawaii-headquartered customers; and
- acquired-company customers.

Geography should also be cross-tabulated with industry. A client base nominally diversified across thousands of employers may still be highly correlated through tourism, construction, real estate, food service, and discretionary spending.

# 6. Acquisitions and ProCare separation

## 6.1 Acquisition thesis

Public references indicate that ProService has pursued acquisitions or adjacent-company combinations. The strategic logic could include:

- adding WSEs;
- acquiring specialized compliance knowledge;
- gaining mainland footprints;
- adding technology;
- broadening benefits administration;
- acquiring sales channels;
- increasing carrier scale; or
- purchasing talent.

However, public announcements alone do not prove successful integration.

## 6.2 Acquisition diligence framework

For every acquired company, the board should receive:

| Category | Required information |
|---|---|
| Transaction | Date, seller, enterprise value, earnout, rollover, financing |
| Strategic thesis | Geography, product, technology, customer, or talent rationale |
| Scale at signing | Clients, WSEs, revenue, gross profit, EBITDA, staff |
| Current result | Retained clients, WSEs, gross profit, staff, realized synergies |
| Integration | Systems, contracts, bank accounts, carrier arrangements, brand |
| Risk | Claims tail, tax exposure, litigation, cyber history, service backlog |
| Return | Actual versus underwritten MOIC and IRR |

## 6.3 ProCare

Public references to **ProCare** require careful treatment. The name may refer to a healthcare-administration or adjacent-services activity associated with ProService and later separated or spun out.

The public record, without transaction documents, is insufficient to conclude:

- whether ProCare was a subsidiary, division, brand, or related-party company;
- what services it provided;
- who owned it before and after separation;
- whether it shared employees, systems, customer data, or facilities;
- whether cash consideration was paid;
- whether management retained interests;
- whether transition-service agreements remain;
- whether ProService customers were transferred;
- or whether the separation removed risk, growth, or both.

The board should treat the separation as a related-party and perimeter-control diligence item until a transaction binder is reviewed.

## 6.4 Strategic interpretation

A ProCare separation could indicate any of the following:

1. simplification of the core PEO;
2. removal of a lower-margin business;
3. divestiture of regulatory complexity;
4. creation of an independent growth company;
5. separation ahead of a sponsor transaction;
6. management or founder liquidity;
7. allocation of technology or intellectual property; or
8. a channel partnership retained outside consolidated ownership.

These are hypotheses only.

# 14. Hawaii competitive position

## 14.1 Sources of advantage

ProService’s likely advantages are:

1. **Scale:** Thousands of employer clients and tens of thousands of WSEs.
2. **Local regulatory depth:** Hawaii-specific benefits and employment rules.
3. **Brand trust:** A long operating history.
4. **Service density:** Local teams and relationships.
5. **Risk data:** Historical payroll, claims, and customer-service information.
6. **Purchasing scale:** Benefits and insurance leverage.
7. **Switching costs:** Payroll, employee records, benefits, and policies are difficult to migrate.
8. **Distribution:** Relationships with advisers and community institutions.
9. **Bundling:** One accountable provider across multiple HR functions.

## 14.2 Competitive groups

The relevant competitors are broader than direct PEOs:

- national PEOs such as ADP TotalSource, TriNet, Insperity, and Paychex;
- payroll and HCM providers;
- local payroll bureaus;
- benefits brokers;
- insurance agencies;
- HR consultants;
- outsourced accounting firms;
- vertical software platforms;
- internal HR hires; and
- “do nothing” or manual administration.

## 14.3 Market-share caution

A claim such as “largest PEO in Hawaii” does not disclose:

- share of addressable employers;
- share of WSEs;
- share of PEO gross profit;
- island-by-island penetration;
- segment penetration;
- or share relative to employers that do not use a PEO.

The total addressable market should be built from Hawaii establishment counts, employment by company size, eligible payroll, and PEO propensity—not from a national market multiple.

# 17. Strategic implications for a new CEO

## 17.1 First 30 days: establish truth

The new CEO should create a single operating fact book covering:

- legal entities;
- ownership;
- debt;
- customers;
- WSEs;
- products;
- gross billings;
- revenue;
- gross profit;
- EBITDA;
- retention;
- insurance exposure;
- technology;
- service quality;
- talent;
- and geographic concentration.

Every metric should have a definition, owner, system of record, reconciliation, and historical series.

## 17.2 First 60 days: protect the franchise

Immediate risk review should cover:

- payroll-funding controls;
- tax remittance;
- client-money segregation;
- cyber posture;
- disaster recovery;
- benefit eligibility and reconciliation;
- workers’ compensation reserves;
- captive solvency;
- carrier concentration;
- top-client concentration;
- executive succession;
- and re-platforming stability.

The objective is to prevent a low-frequency event from damaging the company’s core trust asset.

## 17.3 First 90 days: choose value-creation priorities

Potential value-creation levers should be ranked by risk-adjusted return:

1. improve retention;
2. reduce implementation friction;
3. increase product penetration;
4. reprice uneconomic clients;
5. lower avoidable service demand;
6. improve claims and safety outcomes;
7. complete technology migration;
8. deploy governed AI;
9. sharpen vertical segmentation;
10. strengthen channels;
11. selectively consolidate Hawaii adjacencies;
12. test mainland propositions; and
13. rationalize non-core entities.

## 17.4 Strategic principle: defend Hawaii before exporting

The Hawaii franchise is probably the company’s highest-quality asset. The CEO should not trade its service quality for speculative mainland growth.

Expansion should be funded and governed as a portfolio of experiments with clear kill criteria. Hawaii service metrics should be a constraint: if mainland investment causes deterioration at home, the expansion pace is too fast.

## 17.5 Strategic principle: sell outcomes, not administrative tasks

The strongest proposition is not “we process payroll.” It is:

- fewer compliance failures;
- predictable labor administration;
- better access to benefits;
- safer workplaces;
- faster hiring and onboarding;
- less management distraction;
- and greater employee stability.

Product design, pricing, and sales compensation should align to those outcomes.

## 17.6 Strategic principle: make insurance economics explicit

Benefits and workers’ compensation can create competitive advantage and profit, but can also conceal volatility.

The CEO should separate:

- service economics;
- brokerage or commission economics;
- underwriting or retained-risk economics;
- reserve development;
- and float income.

Normalized EBITDA should not include favorable reserve movements as if they were recurring service margin.

## 17.7 Strategic principle: use AI to augment trust

AI should first improve internal accuracy, speed, and knowledge access. It should not autonomously make sensitive employment decisions or provide unreviewed legal guidance.

The winning model is likely:

- AI handles classification, retrieval, summarization, and anomaly detection;
- trained specialists exercise judgment;
- customers retain access to accountable humans;
- and every use case has measurable economics and controls.

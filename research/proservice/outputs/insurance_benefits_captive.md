## Executive conclusion

Benefits and workers’ compensation should not be treated as ordinary “pass-through” components of a PEO offering. They are a combination of:

1. **Customer acquisition and retention products;**
2. **Large-volume cash and reconciliation systems;**
3. **Potential sources of administrative revenue and purchasing leverage;**
4. **Tail-risk businesses whose apparent current-period margin can be distorted by timing, reserves, recoveries, collateral, or accounting presentation; and**
5. **Regulated promises that may survive customer churn and continue producing liabilities long after the related service revenue ends.**

For ProService, four board-level conclusions follow.

### 1. Hawaii’s regulatory structure makes compliant health-benefit administration unusually valuable

Hawaii’s Prepaid Health Care Act generally requires covered employers to furnish qualifying health coverage to employees who work at least 20 hours a week after the applicable eligibility period. The statute also limits the employee’s contribution, generally to the lesser of one-half of the premium or 1.5% of wages, subject to statutory details and exemptions.

That framework raises the administrative cost of operating in Hawaii. It also increases the value of accurate payroll-benefit integration. A PEO that can determine eligibility, calculate employee contribution caps, reconcile enrollment and payroll, collect customer funds, and transmit carrier premiums accurately can offer customers a genuine operational advantage.

The corresponding risk is that a PEO can become the point at which payroll, carrier enrollment, customer billing, statutory eligibility, leave status, and termination data conflict. Small error rates applied to a large participant base can create material receivables, retroactive premiums, employee claims, customer disputes, or regulatory exposure.

### 2. HMSA access can be commercially important without implying that ProService owns health-insurance risk

Public marketing references to HMSA do **not**, by themselves, establish that ProService:

- underwrites medical claims;
- receives medical-loss-ratio profit;
- has a guaranteed rate advantage;
- owns an exclusive HMSA distribution relationship;
- receives experience refunds;
- holds reserves for incurred medical claims; or
- may move carrier premiums freely between customers or plans.

The most likely outside-in interpretation is that ProService provides access to, enrolls participants in, bills for, and administers one or more HMSA products under a group, association, master-policy, agency, or service arrangement.

That inference must be tested against the actual contracts. The economics can range from a transparent administrative fee with carrier premiums passed through as agent to a bundled gross-price model with renewal, collection, minimum-participation, retrospective adjustment, or bad-debt exposure. Contract language—not marketing terminology—determines the economics and accounting.

### 3. Workers’ compensation can produce durable advantage, but only if loss emergence and capital costs are measured correctly

A PEO can influence workers’ compensation results through:

- customer and class-code selection;
- payroll and exposure accuracy;
- safety engineering;
- injury triage;
- provider direction where lawful;
- return-to-work programs;
- claims advocacy;
- fraud identification;
- reserve review;
- carrier/reinsurer negotiation; and
- customer-level pricing and remediation.

The danger is that reported margin may precede ultimate loss emergence. A favorable current accident year can deteriorate through medical inflation, reopened claims, permanent-disability development, litigation, reserve strengthening, or disputed reinsurance recoveries.

The board should therefore reject any workers’ compensation profitability measure that omits:

- paid losses;
- case reserves;
- incurred-but-not-reported reserves;
- unallocated and allocated loss-adjustment expense;
- retrospective premium adjustments;
- reinsurance cost;
- reinstatement premium;
- collateral;
- captive operating expense;
- fronting fees;
- taxes and assessments;
- capital charges; and
- prior-year development.

### 4. A captive is not automatically a profit center

A captive may reduce frictional insurance costs, improve claims-data ownership, retain predictable layers, and align prevention with economics. It can also convert an insurer’s quoted premium into:

- funded loss reserves;
- regulatory capital;
- collateral;
- reinsurance premium;
- fronting fees;
- actuarial and audit expense;
- investment constraints; and
- remote tail liabilities.

The correct question is not, “Does the captive make money?” It is:

> After ultimate losses, operating costs, fronting, reinsurance, collateral, taxes, and a risk-appropriate charge for trapped capital, does retained risk create superior risk-adjusted economics across a complete insurance cycle?

# I. Analytical framework

This report uses three labels:

- **FACT:** A statement grounded in identified public law, regulation, filing, or company material.
- **INFERENCE:** An outside-in conclusion that appears reasonable but requires internal validation.
- **ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL:** Hypothetical figures used to show economic sensitivity. They are not estimates of ProService’s actual results.

The most important diligence principle is to separate three businesses that may be presented together commercially:

1. **PEO administration:** payroll, HR, compliance, eligibility, enrollment, reconciliation, safety, and claims support;
2. **Insurance distribution or access:** placing or administering third-party carrier products; and
3. **Risk assumption:** retaining health, workers’ compensation, EPLI, or other loss variability directly or through a captive, deductible, reimbursement agreement, retrospective-rating arrangement, or contractual indemnity.

Only the first two can safely be assessed from customer-facing descriptions. The third requires contracts, actuarial work, claims triangles, regulatory filings, and cash/collateral schedules.

# II. Hawaii Prepaid Health Care Act

## A. Core mechanics

**FACT.** Hawaii enacted its Prepaid Health Care Act in 1974. The governing provisions are in [Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 393](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/hrscurrent/Vol07_Ch0346-0398/HRS0393/). The Hawaii Department of Labor and Industrial Relations administers the program through its Disability Compensation Division; DLIR’s public program materials are available through its [Prepaid Health Care page](https://labor.hawaii.gov/dcd/about-phc/).

The principal mechanics relevant to a PEO are:

- Covered employees generally become eligible when they work at least 20 hours per week for the required period.
- Employers must furnish a qualifying health plan unless an exemption applies.
- The employee share is generally capped at the lesser of:
  - one-half of the premium; or
  - 1.5% of the employee’s wages.
- The employer bears the balance.
- Hawaii approves qualifying prepaid health-care plans.
- Exemptions and coordination rules can apply, including where an employee receives qualifying coverage through another source.
- Eligibility, termination, leave, hours, wages, and plan status must be administered consistently.

Legal conclusions should be confirmed against current HRS Chapter 393, applicable Hawaii Administrative Rules, DLIR guidance, and counsel’s interpretation.

### Employer-contribution example

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

Assume:

- Monthly plan premium: **$900**
- Employee monthly wages: **$3,000**
- Half of premium: **$450**
- 1.5% of wages: **$45**

The employee’s permitted share would generally be capped at **$45**, and the employer would bear **$855**, before considering plan-specific rules or exemptions.

If wages are **$8,000**:

- 1.5% of wages: **$120**
- Half of premium: **$450**

The employee share would generally be capped at **$120**, leaving **$780** to the employer.

Thus, unlike a conventional “employer pays 50%” assumption, the wage-based limitation can shift most of the premium cost to the employer.

## B. Why payroll integration matters

The statutory formula links benefit administration directly to hours and wages. This is a natural PEO advantage because the same system can potentially hold:

- payroll frequency;
- gross wages;
- hours worked;
- employment status;
- eligibility date;
- benefit election;
- employee contribution;
- employer contribution; and
- carrier enrollment status.

A standalone broker may arrange a plan but not own the payroll calculations that determine whether employee deductions comply with Hawaii law.

**INFERENCE.** ProService’s strongest defensible benefit advantage is more likely to be **integrated compliance and administration** than pure purchasing scale. Scale may help, but the durable asset is the ability to make payroll, eligibility, billing, carrier, and statutory data agree.

## C. High-risk edge cases

The diligence team should sample:

- fluctuating-hour employees crossing 20 hours;
- mid-pay-period hires and terminations;
- retroactive payroll corrections;
- unpaid leave;
- workers’ compensation leave;
- employees with more than one employer;
- employees claiming coverage elsewhere;
- union employees;
- seasonal employees;
- multiple payroll frequencies;
- dependent additions and removals;
- divorce and qualified-status events;
- late customer funding;
- late carrier enrollment;
- retroactive terminations;
- continuation coverage;
- insufficient net pay for deductions; and
- customer migrations onto or away from the PEO platform.

A compliance rate expressed only as a percentage can conceal significance. If 99.5% of 40,000 monthly participant records are correct, 200 records per month remain wrong. The dollar and employee-impact distribution of those exceptions matters more than the headline percentage.

## C. Sources of PEO advantage

A PEO may produce value in six ways:

### 1. Aggregated administration

One standardized connection to a large PEO may be easier for a carrier than thousands of small-employer interfaces.

### 2. Reduced transaction cost

Integrated payroll can reduce missing deductions, manual invoices, eligibility mismatches, and fragmented remittance.

### 3. Improved small-employer access

Customers may obtain plan choices, service levels, and enrollment infrastructure they could not efficiently build alone.

### 4. Compliance support

The PEO can operationalize the Prepaid Health Care Act’s eligibility and contribution calculations.

### 5. Lower customer volatility

A carrier’s relationship with an aggregated platform may persist even as individual customers enter and leave.

### 6. Better customer experience

A single service model for payroll and benefits can shorten issue resolution.

None of these necessarily means the PEO obtains a lower **medical-risk price**. Carrier rates still reflect law, approved rating methods, benefit design, population, geography, utilization, and market structure.

# IV. Health-plan economics and accounting

## A. Gross versus net presentation

The key accounting issue is whether ProService controls the promised insurance or benefit service before it is transferred, or instead arranges for another party to provide it. The analysis should follow the principal-versus-agent framework in ASC 606, including control, primary responsibility, pricing discretion, and inventory/risk indicators.

### Example

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

- Customer billed for health coverage: **$100 million**
- Amount remitted to carriers: **$94 million**
- Explicit benefit administration revenue: **$4 million**
- Other adjustments: **$2 million**

Two presentations might appear economically similar but look radically different:

- **Gross presentation:** $100 million revenue and $94 million carrier cost;
- **Net presentation:** $6 million revenue.

Gross margin percentage would be 6% in the first case and 100% before operating expense in the second. Neither tells the board whether ProService retains medical risk.

The accounting memorandum should separately address:

- carrier premium;
- explicit administration;
- broker commissions;
- customer service fees;
- credits;
- retroactive adjustments;
- bad debt;
- rebates;
- experience refunds;
- stop-loss recoveries;
- state or federal assessments; and
- amounts collected before coverage periods.

## B. Benefit-cost risk

Potential loss channels include:

- fixed customer price versus variable carrier premium;
- customer nonpayment while carrier liability continues;
- enrollment errors;
- ineligible participants;
- retroactive additions;
- delayed terminations;
- underbilling;
- contribution-cap errors;
- carrier recoupments;
- implementation credits;
- waived fees;
- duplicate records;
- unreconciled cash;
- incorrect coverage tier;
- dependent-verification errors; and
- customer credits not matched to carrier credits.

### Sensitivity model

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

Assume annual carrier premium of **$240 million** and a PEO’s net administrative economics before exceptions of **$12 million**.

- A **0.25%** unrecovered billing/eligibility leakage equals **$600,000**, or 5% of the nominal $12 million economics.
- A **1%** adverse carrier adjustment equals **$2.4 million**, or 20%.
- Thirty days of uncollected customer premium equals roughly **$20 million** of working-capital exposure.

Small basis-point errors matter when applied to premium volume.

## C. Medical-loss-ratio terminology

Federal medical-loss-ratio rules generally require health insurers in applicable markets to spend specified percentages of premium on clinical services and quality-improvement activities, subject to statutory methodology and adjustments. CMS explains the framework through its [Medical Loss Ratio resources](https://www.cms.gov/marketplace/private-health-insurance/medical-loss-ratio).

For a PEO, “loss ratio” can be misleading unless it actually retains medical claims. Management should distinguish:

- insurer statutory MLR;
- plan claims divided by premium;
- claims plus expenses divided by premium;
- customer billing divided by carrier premium;
- benefit gross margin; and
- underwriting gain in any risk-bearing vehicle.

The board should prohibit use of “health loss ratio” without a written numerator, denominator, accounting basis, period, and statement of who legally bears the claims.

A PEO adds complexity because worksite employees remain distributed among client locations even when payroll, coverage, or policy administration is centralized. Correct client-level exposure data is fundamental.

### Reserve-development example

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

Suppose reported annual workers’ compensation economics are:

- Customer charges: **$50 million**
- Carrier/fronting/reinsurance/administrative costs: **$20 million**
- Initially booked ultimate losses: **$24 million**
- Apparent margin: **$6 million**

If ultimate losses later rise by 15%, losses increase by **$3.6 million**, reducing apparent margin to **$2.4 million**. A further **$1 million** dispute over reinsurance recovery reduces it to **$1.4 million** before a capital charge.

The board should see both booked and independently indicated ultimate loss, plus outcomes at the high end of the actuary’s reasonable range.

# VI. Captive structure and economics

## A. Hawaii framework

**FACT.** Hawaii regulates captive insurers through the DCCA Insurance Division. Its [Captive Insurance Branch](https://cca.hawaii.gov/captive/) provides statutory, licensing, filing, and market information. The governing law should be validated against current [Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 431, Article 19](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/hrscurrent/Vol09_Ch0431-0435H/HRS0431/HRS_0431-0019.htm).

Potential structures can include:

- single-parent captive;
- group captive;
- association captive;
- risk-retention structure where legally available;
- protected-cell arrangement;
- rent-a-captive participation;
- deductible reimbursement vehicle;
- direct-writing captive; or
- reinsurance captive behind a licensed fronting carrier.

The legal form does not reveal economic substance. A captive may retain only a narrow layer or a substantial portion of severity and aggregate risk.

## B. Typical flow

A common workers’ compensation captive arrangement can involve:

1. A licensed carrier issues required policies.
2. The carrier collects or is owed premium.
3. The carrier cedes an agreed portion to the captive.
4. The captive reimburses losses within its retained layer.
5. Third-party reinsurance protects specified per-occurrence or aggregate layers.
6. The fronting carrier requires collateral because it remains obligated to claimants if the captive fails.
7. The captive holds reserves and capital under regulatory and actuarial oversight.

The board must map cash, legal obligations, accounting, and collateral separately.

## C. Capital and collateral

A captive can consume capital through:

- statutory minimum capital;
- risk-based regulatory expectations;
- adverse-development buffers;
- letters of credit;
- trust assets;
- funds withheld;
- fronting-carrier collateral;
- reinsurer security requirements;
- liquidity reserves; and
- restricted investment portfolios.

Collateral can exceed booked GAAP reserves because the carrier may use a more conservative confidence level, include unearned exposure, impose contractual add-ons, or refuse credit for disputed recoverables.

### Full economic return

A proper captive return calculation is:

**Premium or risk charge received**  
**+ investment income**  
**+ recoveries**  
**− ultimate losses and LAE**  
**− fronting cost**  
**− reinsurance cost**  
**− operating expense**  
**− taxes and assessments**  
**− collateral expense**  
**− expected credit loss**  
**− charge for committed capital**  
**= risk-adjusted captive value creation**

### Capital-charge example

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

Assume:

- Captive underwriting gain before capital charge: **$5 million**
- Restricted capital and collateral attributable to program: **$50 million**
- Required pre-tax return: **12%**
- Capital charge: **$6 million**

The program destroys **$1 million** of economic value despite reporting a $5 million accounting gain before capital cost.

## D. Reinsurance risks

The board should review:

- attachment points;
- limits;
- aggregate protection;
- exclusions;
- occurrence definitions;
- communicable-disease language;
- terrorism;
- clash exposure;
- reinstatements;
- notice requirements;
- claims-control clauses;
- commutation;
- credit for reinsurance;
- collectability;
- disputes;
- collateral;
- reinsurer ratings; and
- concentration.

A reinsurance asset is not cash. Recoveries can be delayed or denied by late notice, coverage disputes, allocation disagreements, aggregation issues, documentation defects, or insolvency.

## E. Governance thresholds

Suggested board thresholds—not legal requirements—include:

| Metric | Green | Amber | Red |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Captive capital above board minimum | >125% | 110–125% | <110% |
| Collateral headroom | >20% | 10–20% | <10% |
| Largest net loss / available captive capital | <10% | 10–20% | >20% |
| Unsecured recoverables / capital | <10% | 10–25% | >25% |
| Adverse prior-year development / opening reserves | <3% | 3–7% | >7% |
| High-hazard payroll growth | <10% | 10–20% | >20% |
| Receivables over 60 days / annual program charges | <0.5% | 0.5–1.5% | >1.5% |
| One carrier or reinsurer share of protection | <35% | 35–50% | >50% |
| Stress capital after 1-in-100 scenario | Above minimum + buffer | Above legal minimum only | Below minimum |

Actual limits should be calibrated to actuarial volatility, risk appetite, liquidity, and contractual collateral triggers.

# VII. EPLI and insurance adjacencies

PEOs can potentially distribute or support:

- employment practices liability insurance;
- cyber insurance;
- fiduciary liability;
- employee dishonesty/crime;
- business-owner policies;
- general liability;
- management liability;
- paid-leave or statutory disability products;
- life and disability;
- accident;
- dental and vision; and
- supplemental benefits.

EPLI is strategically adjacent because the PEO may provide handbooks, HR advice, training, investigations, and termination guidance. That creates both value and correlated risk.

Key questions include:

- Who is insured: PEO, client, worksite employee, or all three?
- Are co-employment claims covered?
- Are wage-and-hour claims excluded?
- Is defense inside or outside limits?
- Does HR advice create an exclusion or professional-liability gap?
- Who controls counsel?
- How are multiple clients aggregated?
- Is there a common-cause or batch provision?
- Are punitive damages insurable?
- What tail applies after client termination?
- Does the PEO indemnify clients beyond insurance coverage?

No adjacency should enter a captive merely because it is easy to sell. It should enter only when exposure data are credible, coverage wording is stable, losses are sufficiently predictable, and tail correlation with the core business is acceptable.

2. Customer premium cash awaiting remittance;
3. Fiduciary or trust cash;
4. Captive assets supporting reserves;
5. Collateral assets;
6. Funds withheld by carriers;
7. Reinsurance recoverables;
8. Customer receivables;
9. Unearned premiums;
10. Claims-paying cash.

- accounting presentation can distort revenue comparisons.

The best internal benchmark is contribution per worksite employee and customer cohort **after** ultimate insurance losses, cost to serve, bad debt, capital charge, and acquisition cost.

# XII. Downside scenarios

## Scenario 1: HMSA repricing or contract reset

**ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL.**

A 4% carrier increase cannot immediately be passed through on $240 million of annual premium. Three months of lag creates approximately **$2.4 million** of cost exposure:

$240 million × 4% × 3/12 = $2.4 million.

Mitigants:

- aligned renewal dates;
- contractual pass-through rights;
- advance notice;
- customer repricing;
- reserves for known adjustments; and
- alternative plan designs.

## Scenario 2: Eligibility-reconciliation failure

A systems migration causes 1% of 30,000 participants to remain enrolled one extra month at an $850 average premium.

Potential gross exposure:

300 × $850 = **$255,000**, before dependents, claims, credits, or recovery from customers.

## Scenario 3: Large workers’ compensation loss

A severe claim pierces the captive retention, triggers reinstatement premium, and causes the fronting carrier to increase collateral. The income statement records the retained loss while liquidity also falls from a collateral call. Reinsurance recovery arrives months later.

This illustrates why solvency, earnings, and liquidity stresses must be modeled separately.

## Scenario 4: Reserve deterioration

Prior accident years strengthen by 8% on $40 million of net reserves, creating **$3.2 million** of adverse development. If customer prices were based on favorable recent loss ratios, renewal margins also compress.

## Scenario 5: Carrier failure or exit

A carrier declines renewal or imposes materially higher collateral. The PEO may face:

- replacement pricing;
- data conversion;
- customer communications;
- policy issuance delays;
- collateral trapped under the old program;
- duplicate collateral; and
- claims-tail administration.

## Scenario 6: Recession and customer failures

Customer payroll contracts, service revenue falls, health-plan participation changes, and receivables deteriorate. Insurance liabilities and carrier-payment obligations may not contract at the same speed.

## Scenario 7: Catastrophic local event

A hurricane, wildfire, earthquake, or infrastructure disruption produces correlated effects:

- work injuries;
- health utilization;
- customer distress;
- payroll disruption;
- claims-service interruption;
- cyber/fraud risk;
- investment volatility; and
- operational concentration.

## Scenario 8: Regulatory reclassification

A regulator determines that an arrangement constitutes insurance administration, premium handling, plan sponsorship, or risk assumption requiring different licensing, reserves, disclosures, or conduct. Remediation may include restitution, revised contracts, capital, or operational separation.

# Final board judgment

ProService’s benefits and risk capabilities could be a meaningful competitive advantage in Hawaii. The same local complexity that burdens small employers creates demand for an integrated PEO capable of translating payroll data into compliant health-benefit and workers’ compensation administration.

But scale alone is not the advantage. The advantage exists only if ProService can demonstrate that:

- eligibility records are accurate;
- employee contributions comply with Hawaii law;
- carrier invoices reconcile to payroll and customer billing;
- customer credit exposure is controlled;
- health-plan accounting reflects economic substance;
- workers’ compensation reserves are independently adequate;
- safety and claims programs improve measured outcomes;
- reinsurance is collectible;
- captive capital is sufficient;
- collateral demands are survivable; and
- risk-adjusted returns exceed the cost of committed capital.

Until the contracts and internal schedules prove otherwise, the board should treat HMSA economics as a carrier-dependent administrative relationship, health premium float as encumbered liquidity, workers’ compensation margin as provisional until losses mature, and captive profits as incomplete until charged for tail risk and capital.

The essential governance rule is:

> **Never allow current-period growth, gross billing volume, favorable reserve development, or captive investment income to substitute for reconciled cash, contract clarity, independently indicated ultimate loss, and stressed capital adequacy.**

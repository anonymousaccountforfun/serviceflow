## Executive conclusions

The PEO industry is best understood not as outsourced payroll software, but as a **regulated employment-risk and benefits-financing platform wrapped around recurring HR administration**. Its strategic value comes from pooling, specialization, compliance infrastructure, and the ability to spread fixed expertise over thousands of small employers. Its principal risk is the same structure: the PEO receives large payroll and insurance cash flows, assumes or administers obligations that can exceed its visible administrative revenue, and creates correlated exposure across clients.

Eight conclusions matter most:

1. **The economic scale of a PEO cannot be read from “revenue” alone.** Gross payroll, gross billings, GAAP revenue, net service revenue, gross profit, and adjusted EBITDA describe different layers of the same system. Boards should normalize every competitor into a common waterfall before comparing scale or margins.

2. **PEOs serve a large but still underpenetrated market.** NAPEO’s established industry estimates place the sector at roughly 500 PEOs, more than 200,000 client businesses, and approximately 4.5 million worksite employees. Depending on the denominator, this implies approximately 15%–17% penetration of the employer firms or workers conventionally considered PEO-addressable—not 15% of the entire U.S. workforce.

3. **The structural demand drivers remain favorable.** Small employers face increasingly complex payroll tax, leave, wage-hour, privacy, retirement, healthcare, workers’ compensation, immigration, and employment-law obligations. Complexity grows faster than their internal HR capacity.

4. **The product is sticky but not invulnerable.** Payroll conversion, employee disruption, benefit renewals, underwriting data, and compliance dependencies raise switching costs. Yet payroll/HCM platforms, brokers, administrative-services-only arrangements, and software-first PEOs can unbundle the easiest accounts.

5. **Insurance and client credit—not payroll processing—are the existential risks.** A small adverse movement in health cost per worksite employee, workers’ compensation loss development, or payroll-funding collectability can erase a large portion of administrative profit.

6. **Federal CPEO certification is valuable but not a blanket safe harbor.** It can clarify federal employment-tax liability and client tax-credit treatment for qualifying relationships. It does not eliminate state licensing, benefits, wage-hour, ERISA, workers’ compensation, privacy, fiduciary, or contractual risk.

7. **Scale produces both advantage and fragility.** More WSEs support specialized talent, procurement power, actuarial credibility, automation, and distribution. They also increase concentration, systemic cash-flow requirements, data-security exposure, and the dollar impact of small pricing errors.

8. **For ProService, geographic density is an asset.** Hawaii’s distinct healthcare, temporary-disability, prepaid-health, workers’ compensation, tax, labor, and island-operating environment favors a specialist with local density. The strategic danger is not merely a national PEO entering Hawaii; it is a software or broker-led competitor abstracting the customer relationship while outsourcing local complexity.

The board should manage the company as four interlocking businesses:

- a payroll-tax fiduciary and treasury operation;
- an insurance underwriting and benefits-financing operation;
- a recurring HR/compliance services company;
- a distribution and retention platform.

A dashboard that emphasizes WSE count, “revenue,” and EBITDA while omitting payroll float, client credit, health trend, loss development, renewal repricing, and regulatory exceptions is incomplete.

# 1. Industry definition and history

## 1.1 What a PEO is

A professional employer organization enters a contractual relationship with a client employer under which employment responsibilities are allocated between the parties. The PEO generally:

- reports and remits payroll taxes;
- administers payroll and wage payments;
- provides or administers employee benefits;
- maintains workers’ compensation coverage or programs;
- supports HR compliance and employee relations;
- supplies HR technology and records;
- may sponsor benefit plans; and
- may be treated as an employer for specified statutory purposes.

The client generally retains:

- operational control of the business;
- day-to-day direction of employees;
- hiring and termination authority, although the PEO may advise;
- workplace safety obligations;
- product, customer, and professional risk;
- responsibility for accurately reporting hours, compensation, and worker status; and
- business-specific licenses and compliance.

“Co-employment” is useful commercial shorthand, but not a universal legal conclusion. Employer status is statute- and fact-specific. A party may be an employer for federal payroll-tax purposes but not for a particular discrimination, wage-hour, benefit, licensing, or tort issue.

A PEO is distinct from:

| Model | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Staffing firm | Staffing firm typically recruits and supplies workers to perform services for customers. |
| Temporary agency | Assignment is generally temporary; agency commonly controls placement. |
| Payroll processor | Processes customer data but ordinarily does not enter a co-employment relationship. |
| ASO | Provides administrative services without assuming the full PEO employment/insurance structure. |
| HCM SaaS | Licenses software; customer remains the direct administrator and risk holder. |
| Employer of record | Usually becomes legal employer where the customer lacks an employing entity or wishes to employ in another jurisdiction. |
| PEO | Client already employs its workforce operationally; responsibilities are contractually allocated to the PEO. |

## 1.2 Historical development

The industry evolved from employee-leasing arrangements in the 1970s and 1980s. Early growth exposed ambiguity over tax responsibility, benefit-plan eligibility, workers’ compensation, and whether leasing arrangements were being used to manipulate insurance classifications or pension rules.

The modern PEO model emerged through:

1. **State recognition and licensing.** States developed registration, financial-responsibility, contract, workers’ compensation, and disclosure rules.
2. **Industry standardization.** NAPEO promoted the PEO label and model legislation.
3. **Accreditation.** ESAC developed financial and operational assurance programs.
4. **Federal tax clarification.** The Small Business Efficiency Act of 2014 established the IRS CPEO framework in Internal Revenue Code §§3511 and 7705.
5. **Platform expansion.** PEOs added benefits, HRIS, analytics, recruiting, retirement, compliance, and employee self-service.
6. **Software-first competition.** Cloud-native entrants simplified onboarding and integrated payroll, benefits, devices, identity, and global employment.
7. **Consolidation.** National providers and private-equity-backed platforms acquired local PEOs, books of business, and insurance programs.

The industry’s direction has been from fragmented payroll administration toward regulated multi-product employment infrastructure.

# 2. Mechanics of co-employment

## 2.1 Contractual allocation

A sound client-service agreement should state:

- each party’s employer responsibilities;
- who pays wages and from whose account;
- tax reporting and remittance responsibilities;
- benefit-plan sponsorship and eligibility;
- workers’ compensation allocation;
- hiring, discipline, supervision, and termination rights;
- workplace-safety duties;
- data accuracy obligations;
- indemnities and liability caps;
- funding deadlines and debit authorization;
- collateral and credit remedies;
- audit rights;
- dispute resolution;
- treatment at termination;
- continuation and runout obligations; and
- what happens if the client fails to fund payroll.

Contracts do not override statutes. Poor operational behavior can also undermine favorable contractual language.

## 2.2 The operating cycle

A typical cycle is:

1. Client reports employees, hours, pay rates, deductions, locations, classifications, and changes.
2. PEO calculates gross-to-net payroll and associated taxes.
3. PEO invoices or debits the client for payroll, taxes, benefits, insurance, and fees.
4. PEO pays employees and remits taxes and deductions.
5. PEO records benefit and workers’ compensation obligations.
6. PEO reconciles cash, tax accounts, carrier bills, eligibility, and the general ledger.
7. Exceptions flow to HR, tax, insurance, credit, or legal teams.

The period between committing to payroll and collecting good funds is a credit exposure. If the PEO pays employees or authorities before irrevocable client funding, it has effectively extended a short-duration, high-severity loan.

## 2.3 Responsibility matrix

| Area | PEO role | Client role | Principal failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payroll | Calculation, payment, records | Accurate/timely inputs and funding | Underpayment, duplicate pay, failed funding |
| Payroll tax | Filing, deposit, reconciliation | Accurate worker/pay data | Penalties, successor liability, account mismatch |
| Benefits | Plan/admin/enrollment | Eligibility and employee-status data | Ineligible enrollment, missed enrollment, fiduciary dispute |
| Workers’ comp | Policy/program, claims coordination | Safe workplace, reporting, classification accuracy | Misclassification, delayed claim, adverse development |
| HR | Guidance, forms, case support | Final workplace decisions | Joint-employer or discrimination claim |
| Wage-hour | Tools and advice | Scheduling, duties, time accuracy | Off-clock work, exemption error |
| Cyber/privacy | Platform and controls | Authorized users and data hygiene | Payroll diversion, identity theft |
| Treasury | Funding and disbursement | Good funds by deadline | Client default or fraud |

# 3. Service stack and revenue architecture

## 3.1 Service stack

A full-service PEO commonly combines:

- payroll calculation and payment;
- payroll-tax filing and remittance;
- HRIS and employee self-service;
- onboarding and electronic records;
- handbooks and compliance support;
- benefits procurement, enrollment, billing, and COBRA;
- workers’ compensation coverage and claims support;
- retirement-plan access and administration;
- recruiting and performance tools;
- time and attendance;
- training and safety;
- unemployment-claims administration;
- employee-relations advice;
- EPLI access;
- analytics and benchmarking.

The product has three economic layers:

1. **Administrative fee:** PEPM, per-payroll, percentage-of-payroll, or bundled.
2. **Insurance economics:** health, dental, disability, workers’ compensation, and related spreads, commissions, fees, or risk-bearing results.
3. **Ancillary services and float:** retirement, recruiting, timekeeping, compliance, interest, and other services.

## 3.2 The definition problem

Industry comparisons fail when unlike metrics are treated as equivalent.

| Measure | Definition | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| Gross payroll | Employee gross wages processed | Operational and fiduciary scale |
| Gross billings | Payroll plus taxes, benefits, insurance, fees, and adjustments billed to clients | Cash-flow and credit scale |
| GAAP revenue | Amount recognized under applicable principal-versus-agent judgments | Financial reporting |
| Net service/admin revenue | Administrative and other retained economics after pass-throughs | Pricing and business-model comparison |
| Insurance revenue | Premium-equivalent or retained insurance-related revenue, depending on accounting | Insurance scale, only after reconciliation |
| Gross profit | Revenue less direct service/insurance costs | Contribution capacity |
| Operating income | Gross profit less operating expenses | GAAP profitability |
| Adjusted EBITDA | Non-GAAP earnings with company-specific exclusions | Valuation, subject to normalization |
| Free cash flow | Operating cash after capitalized investment and relevant obligations | Debt capacity and resilience |

### Illustrative annual economics for 10,000 WSEs

**Estimate—not an industry benchmark.**

| Item | Assumption | Amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross payroll | $55,000 per WSE | $550.0m |
| Employer taxes and statutory costs | 10% of payroll | $55.0m |
| Health and other benefits billed | $9,000 per WSE | $90.0m |
| Workers’ compensation billed | 1.5% of payroll | $8.25m |
| Administrative/other fees | $2,200 per WSE | $22.0m |
| Approximate gross billings | Sum | $725.25m |

A company could describe itself as processing $550 million of payroll, generating $725 million of billings, recognizing a much smaller or larger GAAP revenue number depending on principal-agent accounting, and retaining only a fraction as gross profit. None of these statements is necessarily inconsistent.

## 3.3 Board reporting waterfall

Every PEO should report:

1. Beginning WSEs;
2. adds;
3. client losses;
4. WSE changes inside retained clients;
5. ending and average WSEs;
6. gross payroll;
7. gross billings;
8. pass-through payroll;
9. taxes and statutory pass-throughs;
10. benefit and insurance billings;
11. net administrative/service revenue;
12. insurance underwriting result;
13. gross profit;
14. sales and service expense;
15. technology and G&A;
16. EBITDA;
17. normalized EBITDA;
18. operating cash flow;
19. collateral and restricted cash;
20. free cash flow.

Acquisition accounting, contingent consideration, stock compensation, implementation costs, capitalized software, insurance reserve changes, and interest on client funds should be disclosed separately.

# 4. Industry size, TAM, SAM, SOM, and adoption

## 4.1 Established industry facts

NAPEO’s widely cited industry research has estimated approximately:

- **500 PEOs;**
- **more than 200,000 client businesses;**
- **approximately 4.5 million worksite employees;**
- **more than $300 billion of annual wages;**
- a PEO footprint larger than several major U.S. employing industries.

These figures should not be combined with Census or BLS figures without aligning year, employer definition, firm versus establishment, WSE coverage, and target employer size.

NAPEO-sponsored work by economist Laurie Bassi has also reported that PEO clients grow faster, have lower employee turnover, and are less likely to go out of business than comparable small firms. Those are important findings, but boards should describe them as **association-sponsored observational research**, not proof that PEO use alone causes the outcome.

## 4.2 Three TAM methods

### A. Worker method

Let:

- addressable private-sector workers at small and midsize firms = 60m–75m;
- plausible mature PEO adoption = 25%–35%;
- retained net economics = $1,800–$3,000 per WSE annually.

Then:

| Case | WSE opportunity | Net economics/WSE | Revenue pool |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Conservative | 15.0m | $1,800 | $27bn |
| Base | 22.5m | $2,300 | $51.8bn |
| High | 26.25m | $3,000 | $78.8bn |

These are strategic estimates, not published market size.

### B. Employer-firm method

Assume:

- 6.0m employer firms;
- 20%–30% fit the operational/industry profile;
- 15–25 average employees;
- $30,000–$60,000 annual retained revenue per client.

This produces approximately 1.2m–1.8m theoretically addressable clients and a $36bn–$108bn retained-revenue pool. The range is deliberately broad because microbusinesses may be uneconomic and larger firms may choose ASO or internal HR.

### C. Current-base expansion method

If the established industry base is roughly 4.5m WSEs and estimated effective addressable penetration is 15%–17%, the implied current denominator is approximately 26m–30m WSEs. Raising penetration to 25% yields roughly 6.5m–7.5m WSEs—an incremental 2m–3m over the established base before labor-force growth.

This narrower method is more defensible for near-term planning than a total-employment TAM.

## 4.3 SAM and SOM for ProService

A Hawaii-focused SAM should be built from state establishment and employment records, not a national market report:

1. Count private employer establishments by county, NAICS, and employee band.
2. Remove prohibited, uninsurable, structurally unattractive, or uneconomic classes.
3. Estimate covered employees, payroll, health eligibility, and workers’ compensation exposure.
4. Adjust for multi-establishment ownership to avoid double counting.
5. Estimate existing PEO penetration and incumbent share.
6. Model win probability by segment and channel.
7. Apply implementation and service-capacity constraints.

**Illustrative methodology only:**

| Funnel | Example |
|---|---:|
| Private-sector employees in selected geography | 500,000 |
| Employees in target size bands | 275,000 |
| After excluded/high-risk industries | 225,000 |
| After economic/benefit-fit screen | 180,000 |
| Estimated current PEO WSEs | 35,000 |
| Unpenetrated plus contestable installed base | 160,000 |
| Five-year executable capture at 8% | 12,800 WSEs |

SOM must reflect sales capacity, implementation slots, broker coverage, underwriting capacity, and retention—not merely market share ambition.

## 4.4 Adoption and growth

The correct adoption denominator depends on the decision:

- **Public policy:** PEO WSEs divided by total private employment.
- **Industry penetration:** PEO WSEs divided by workers in genuinely addressable firm sizes and industries.
- **Sales planning:** eligible employers or employees in served geographies.
- **Competitive share:** provider WSEs divided by PEO WSEs in the same market.
- **Product penetration:** enrolled employees divided by eligible WSEs.

Historical NAPEO research indicates substantial long-run industry growth and reports PEO employment growth exceeding that of U.S. employment. Boards should not mechanically extrapolate an old CAGR into 2026–2030. Growth now depends on:

- small-business formation and failure;
- employment growth within clients;
- benefits inflation;
- regulation;
- carrier capacity;
- digital acquisition costs;
- national-provider pricing;
- software substitution;
- M&A;
- recession severity.

A reasonable **scenario range**, rather than a claimed forecast, is **3%–10% annual WSE growth** for a healthy provider, split among new logo growth, retained-client employment change, acquisition, and attrition.

# 5. Segmentation

## 5.1 Employer size

| Client size | Economics | Needs | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 employees | Low absolute revenue; high setup cost | Payroll, basic compliance, benefits access | Churn, owner dependency, adverse selection |
| 5–19 | Core PEO fit | HR leverage, benefits, compliance | Credit and concentration by owner |
| 20–49 | Attractive service leverage | HR depth, recruiting, safety | More complex cases and plan design |
| 50–99 | Higher ACV; stronger buyer sophistication | Analytics, integrations, dedicated HR | ACA complexity and competitive RFPs |
| 100–499 | Large revenue, lower unit service cost if standardized | Custom reporting, integrations, governance | Price pressure, self-insurance/ASO alternatives |
| 500+ | Often ASO/HCM territory | Enterprise controls | Concentration and bespoke complexity |

The “sweet spot” is not universal. It depends on service automation, benefit pricing, sales channel, and local labor law.

## 5.2 Industry

Attractive sectors usually have:

- stable employment;
- predictable payroll;
- low occupational hazard;
- adequate margins;
- meaningful HR complexity;
- limited seasonal volatility.

Higher-risk sectors include:

- construction and roofing;
- transportation;
- staffing;
- agriculture;
- hospitality with high turnover;
- home healthcare;
- businesses with disputed contractor models;
- cannabis and other federally sensitive activities;
- firms with tip, piece-rate, or complex wage practices.

High risk does not mean “never serve.” It means pricing, expertise, collateral, coverage, and appetite must be explicit.

## 5.3 Geography

PEO economics are geographically sensitive because of:

- state licensing;
- unemployment-tax structures;
- wage and leave law;
- workers’ compensation rules;
- local health markets;
- provider networks;
- tax-account transfer rules;
- employment-practices environment;
- payroll-funding mechanics.

Hawaii is particularly distinctive due to its Prepaid Health Care Act, Temporary Disability Insurance requirements, island geography, concentrated provider landscape, high living costs, tourism exposure, and state-specific employment practices. Local regulatory and relationship competence can outweigh generic national scale.

# 6. Competitive landscape

## 6.1 Archetypes

| Archetype | Examples | Advantage | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| National scaled PEO | ADP TotalSource, TriNet, Insperity, Paychex | Brand, capital, technology, specialist depth | Standardization, local-service gaps |
| Regional PEO | Multi-state regional operators | Relationships and flexibility | Lower purchasing and technology scale |
| Local specialist | ProService Hawaii-type model | Local regulation, density, trust | Geographic concentration |
| Vertical PEO | Industry-specialized providers | Underwriting and workflow expertise | Sector concentration |
| Software-first PEO | Rippling, Justworks and peers | UX, integrations, rapid onboarding | Service depth and insurance maturity may vary |
| Broker-led solution | Brokerage plus HR/benefit administration | Trusted adviser and carrier access | Fragmented accountability |
| HCM/payroll substitute | Gusto, UKG, Paylocity, ADP Workforce Now, Paycom | Lower cost, employer retains control | Customer retains compliance and insurance burden |
| ASO | PEO-provided or independent | Flexible, avoids co-employment | Less pooling/risk transfer |
| Internal HR | In-house team | Control and customization | Fixed cost and key-person risk |

Public disclosures from ADP, Paychex, Insperity, and TriNet are valuable, but accounting differs. TriNet has historically reported large insurance-service revenues; Insperity emphasizes worksite employees paid and adjusted EBITDA; ADP and Paychex embed PEO within broader payroll/HCM businesses. Comparisons require segment-level reconciliation.

## 6.2 Porter Five Forces

### Rivalry — **High**

- recurring revenue attracts capital;
- products appear similar in RFPs;
- renewals create annual repricing contests;
- brokers can move books;
- national firms cross-subsidize acquisition;
- software firms compete on user experience.

Counterweights are switching friction, local expertise, claims history, and service relationships.

### Buyer power — **Moderate to high**

Power rises with client size, broker representation, clean claims history, simple operations, and renewal timing. It falls when the client has multi-state complexity, poor loss experience, urgent compliance needs, or deeply embedded workflows.

### Supplier power — **High**

Critical suppliers include health carriers, stop-loss markets, workers’ compensation carriers, banking partners, payroll rails, cloud providers, and scarce HR/actuarial talent. A carrier exit or capacity restriction can impair growth even when customer demand is strong.

### Threat of entrants — **Moderate**

Payroll software is easier to build than a trustworthy PEO. Barriers include licensing, CPEO requirements, carrier relationships, capital, reserves, tax operations, fraud controls, and credibility. Software-first firms can enter by partnering for regulated components.

### Threat of substitutes — **High and rising**

ASO, payroll SaaS, brokers, HR consultants, benefit platforms, and outsourced specialists can reproduce individual components. The PEO’s defense is the integrated outcome: one accountable system, pooled expertise, superior risk control, and measurable employer performance.

# 7. Christensen disruption analysis

Software-first entrants often begin with customers underserved by traditional PEOs: tiny, technology-comfortable companies wanting transparent pricing and self-service. Their product may initially offer less consultative depth but exceed incumbents on ease, integration, and speed.

The disruption path is:

1. automate micro and simple accounts;
2. accumulate employee, payroll, and benefit data;
3. improve compliance and service workflows;
4. add brokers, advisers, and specialists;
5. move upmarket through integrations and modular products;
6. use the system of record to capture adjacent spend.

Incumbents are vulnerable when:

- service is expensive but not differentiated;
- data is fragmented;
- onboarding is manual;
- pricing is opaque;
- product development is constrained by legacy payroll architecture.

However, insurance underwriting, tax operations, local regulation, and complex employee relations resist pure software commoditization. The likely winner is not “software instead of service,” but **software-amplified expert service**.

ProService’s response should be:

- digitize routine work aggressively;
- keep named human accountability for consequential cases;
- encode Hawaii-specific expertise into workflows;
- expose integrations and APIs;
- quantify customer outcomes;
- offer PEO and ASO paths;
- avoid competing solely on a generic HRIS checklist.

# 8. Certification, accreditation, and regulation

## 8.1 Federal CPEO framework

The Small Business Efficiency Act added IRC §§3511 and 7705. IRS certification requires, among other matters:

- tax compliance;
- background and suitability review;
- financial statements;
- bonding;
- quarterly assertions and attestations;
- ongoing reporting;
- use of approved agreements and procedures.

For covered remuneration under a qualifying CPEO contract, federal law can treat the CPEO as the employer for specified employment-tax purposes. The framework also addresses wage-base restart and certain client tax-credit concerns.

Certification is not equivalent to government endorsement of every service, financial strength, benefit plan, or employment practice. Loss or suspension of certification would be a severe commercial and operational event.

## 8.2 ESAC and industry accreditation

Employer Services Assurance Corporation accreditation supplies independent financial and operational assurance and may include bonding. Certification, accreditation, and licensing overlap but are not substitutes:

| Credential | Authority | Principal focus |
|---|---|---|
| IRS CPEO | Federal government | Federal employment-tax framework and financial/suitability requirements |
| State PEO license/registration | State regulator | Local financial, contract, reporting, tax, and workers’ compensation rules |
| ESAC accreditation | Independent industry body | Financial/operational assurance |
| Workers’ comp/insurance licensing | State insurance system | Coverage, producer, carrier, rating, and claims rules |

## 8.3 Regulatory map

Relevant regimes include:

- Internal Revenue Code and IRS employment-tax rules;
- Fair Labor Standards Act;
- Family and Medical Leave Act;
- Title VII, ADA, ADEA, and related EEO laws;
- Affordable Care Act employer provisions;
- ERISA, COBRA, HIPAA, and fiduciary rules;
- OSHA;
- USERRA;
- NLRA joint-employer doctrine;
- state wage-hour, leave, privacy, pay-transparency, unemployment, and tax laws;
- insurance and workers’ compensation statutes;
- state PEO licensing laws;
- data-security and breach-notification laws;
- retirement-plan rules, including multiple-employer and pooled-employer arrangements.

The PEO should maintain a regulatory obligation inventory mapping every duty to:

- legal owner;
- operational owner;
- system control;
- evidence;
- testing frequency;
- escalation route;
- board reporting threshold.

# 9. Principal risk domains

## 9.1 Payroll-tax risk

Failure modes include:

- late or incorrect deposits;
- incorrect EIN or account use;
- wage-base errors;
- state account transitions;
- incorrect jurisdiction;
- amended-return backlog;
- client data errors;
- tax notices not resolved;
- fraud or diversion;
- CPEO contract defects.

Controls should include daily funding reconciliation, segregated authorization, tax calendars, notice aging, independent account confirmation, exception dashboards, and disaster recovery.

**Red flags:**

- unreconciled payroll cash older than one business day;
- material tax notices older than 30 days;
- rising amendments per 1,000 WSEs;
- suspense-account growth;
- reliance on one individual for tax-release authority;
- payroll released before confirmed client funds outside approved limits.

## 9.2 Health-benefit risk

Economic structures range from fully insured to various risk-sharing or self-funded arrangements. The board must understand who bears:

- claim risk;
- trend;
- large claims;
- runout;
- reserve development;
- network changes;
- pharmacy rebates;
- stop-loss exclusions;
- eligibility errors.

### Key health equations

\[
\text{Medical loss ratio} =
\frac{\text{incurred claims + defined quality expenses}}
{\text{premium-equivalent revenue}}
\]

\[
\text{Health contribution per enrolled employee} =
\text{health billings}
-\text{claims/premiums}
-\text{direct administration}
-\text{commissions}
-\text{reserve movement}
\]

Claims per member per month should be decomposed into:

- unit cost;
- utilization;
- severity;
- specialty drugs;
- high-cost claimants;
- demographic and geographic mix;
- plan design;
- network;
- completion factors.

A 200-basis-point deterioration on $100 million of premium-equivalent volume costs $2 million. If administrative EBITDA is $15 million, that consumes 13% of EBITDA.

**Warning thresholds requiring explanation, not universal limits:**

- incurred claim trend > pricing trend by 200 bps;
- MLR > target by 150 bps;
- large-claim frequency > expected by 20%;
- reserve deficiency > 3% of prior estimate;
- enrollment or demographic shift not reflected in renewal pricing;
- material carrier concentration without tested contingency.

## 9.3 Workers’ compensation

Risk is driven by:

- payroll by class code;
- rate;
- experience modification;
- claim frequency;
- claim severity;
- medical inflation;
- litigation;
- reserve development;
- recoveries;
- deductible or retention structure.

Core measures:

\[
\text{Loss ratio} =
\frac{\text{incurred losses + allocated loss adjustment expense}}
{\text{earned premium-equivalent}}
\]

\[
\text{Claim frequency} =
\frac{\text{claims}}{\text{exposure unit}}
\]

Boards should receive accident-year triangles, not merely paid claims. Favorable current cash flow can mask adverse future development.

Red flags include rapid high-hazard growth, class-code overrides, delayed first report of injury, claims-team turnover, adverse prior-year development, and collateral demands rising faster than exposure.

## 9.4 EPLI and employment claims

Exposure arises from:

- discrimination;
- retaliation;
- harassment;
- wrongful termination;
- wage-hour claims;
- leave accommodation;
- background checks;
- pay equity;
- worker classification;
- joint-employer allegations.

EPLI often excludes or limits wage-hour claims, punitive damages, and known circumstances. The PEO should not promise that advice eliminates liability. Advice quality, documentation, escalation, and client compliance matter.

## 9.5 Client credit, fraud, and liquidity

The PEO may owe wages and taxes even when a client fails to fund. Exposure can multiply if failure is detected after multiple payrolls or if a client fabricates deposits.

Risk measures:

- payroll-at-risk before good funds;
- largest unsecured client exposure;
- ACH return rate;
- funding exceptions;
- days sales outstanding;
- collateral coverage;
- client credit migration;
- concentration by industry and owner;
- recovery rate.

A client producing $100,000 of annual service revenue but creating a $1 million payroll loss destroys years of contribution. Sales compensation must not encourage exceptions to credit policy.

**Illustrative controls:**

| Client condition | Possible response |
|---|---|
| Stable, strong credit | Standard prefunding terms |
| Rapid growth or weak liquidity | Earlier debit, reserve, guarantee |
| High payroll concentration | Letter of credit or cash collateral |
| ACH return | Immediate hold and executive review |
| Payroll released without funds | Recorded policy exception and exposure limit |
| Adverse filing/lien | Re-underwrite and reduce limits |

## 9.6 Cyber and privacy

PEOs hold bank accounts, tax identifiers, compensation, medical-benefit information, and identity data. High-impact threats include:

- payroll diversion;
- business-email compromise;
- administrator takeover;
- fraudulent direct-deposit changes;
- ransomware;
- API compromise;
- insider abuse;
- vendor breach.

Minimum controls include phishing-resistant MFA, privileged-access management, dual control, out-of-band verification for bank changes, immutable logs, encryption, segmentation, tested backups, vendor diligence, incident exercises, and cyber insurance.

## 9.7 Regulatory and conduct risk

A PEO can face risk from overstating legal advice, using unlicensed insurance activity, mishandling plan assets, inaccurate marketing claims, inadequate complaint escalation, or permitting aggressive clients to misuse the platform.

The board should track regulatory complaints, reportable events, licenses, examinations, remediation, legal spend, and repeated operational exceptions.

# 10. Unit economics and KPI guardrails

No single “healthy range” applies across accounting models. The following are **diagnostic ranges**, not valuation standards.

| Metric | Healthy directional signal | Risk signal |
|---|---|---|
| Annual gross WSE retention | >85% | <75% |
| Net WSE retention including client growth | >95% | <85% |
| Client retention | >85% | <75% |
| Revenue retention | >90% | <80% |
| Organic WSE growth | >5% | Negative excluding deliberate pruning |
| CAC payback | <24 months | >36 months |
| Gross-profit CAC payback | <30 months | >42 months |
| New-client implementation defects | <2% material-error rate | >5% |
| Payroll accuracy | >99.9% | Repeated material errors |
| On-time payroll/tax funding | >99.9% | Any systemic miss |
| Unsecured payroll exposure | Within approved limit and prefunded norm | Rising faster than WSEs |
| Aged tax notices | Declining; immaterial >30 days | Growing backlog |
| Health pricing minus claim trend | Nonnegative through cycle | Worse than –200 bps |
| Workers’ comp prior-year development | Within actuarial range | Repeated adverse development |
| Largest-client share | <5% preferred | >10% |
| Broker/channel concentration | <20% per source preferred | >30% |
| Service cases reopened | <5% | >10% |
| Employee-service SLA attainment | >90% | <80% |
| Voluntary employee turnover | Below local market | Persistent critical-role loss |
| Liquidity stress coverage | >1.5× modeled need | <1.0× |

Retention must be separated into:

- client-logo retention;
- gross WSE retention;
- WSE change within retained clients;
- acquired WSEs;
- revenue retention;
- gross-profit retention.

A provider can report strong WSE retention while losing profitable clients and adding low-margin workers.

# 11. Sales channels and go-to-market

Principal channels are:

- direct outbound;
- referrals from clients and employees;
- insurance and benefits brokers;
- CPAs and bookkeepers;
- banks and lenders;
- attorneys and HR consultants;
- associations;
- digital inbound;
- payroll/HCM partnerships;
- M&A referral ecosystems.

Channel economics should include:

- lead-to-opportunity conversion;
- opportunity-to-win conversion;
- sales cycle;
- WSEs per win;
- gross profit per win;
- implementation cost;
- commission and residuals;
- first-year retention;
- three-year contribution;
- adverse-selection experience.

Broker business can scale efficiently but creates channel rent and renewal influence. Direct sales offers control but can have high CAC. Referral channels generally produce better trust and retention but are difficult to scale predictably.

Sales governance should prohibit:

- benefit quotes without underwriting approval;
- workers’ compensation classification overrides;
- credit-policy exceptions without authority;
- promises of legal immunity;
- undisclosed multi-year concessions;
- quota credit based solely on WSEs rather than risk-adjusted value.

# 12. Cyclicality

PEOs have mixed cyclicality.

Negative recession effects:

- WSE contraction inside retained clients;
- client failures;
- credit losses;
- reduced hiring;
- price sensitivity;
- payroll-tax complexity;
- adverse mix shifts.

Offsets:

- employers reduce internal HR headcount;
- compliance outsourcing becomes more valuable;
- consolidation opportunities increase;
- claims utilization may change;
- unemployment and workforce restructuring create demand for guidance.

Revenue may fall faster than client count because hours, wages, and employment decline. Conversely, gross billings can rise through inflation even when real service economics weaken.

Stress tests should include:

1. WSEs down 10%;
2. new sales down 30%;
3. client failure rate doubled;
4. health trend 300 bps above pricing;
5. workers’ compensation adverse development;
6. delayed receivables;
7. carrier collateral increase;
8. cyber outage during payroll;
9. simultaneous loss of a major broker or client.

# 13. M&A structure

PEO acquisitions may be structured as:

- stock purchases;
- asset purchases;
- client-book acquisitions;
- renewal-right purchases;
- earnouts based on retained WSEs, revenue, or EBITDA;
- platform-plus-add-on strategies.

Diligence must go beyond normalized EBITDA:

- client contracts and assignment rights;
- retention cohorts;
- payroll-tax accounts and liabilities;
- CPEO status;
- state licenses;
- benefit-plan liabilities and runout;
- workers’ compensation triangles;
- collateral;
- claim reserves;
- client credit;
- wage-hour and employment cases;
- cyber incidents;
- data portability;
- producer commissions;
- revenue recognition;
- related-party transactions;
- quality of earnings;
- working capital;
- ownership of software and data.

The most dangerous deal structure pays for WSEs at close while the buyer inherits adverse claims, weak contracts, unfunded tax issues, or broker-controlled relationships.

Earnouts should reward **retained risk-adjusted gross profit**, not raw WSEs or billings. Integration should sequence tax, treasury, insurance, and payroll controls before cosmetic brand or portal changes.

# 14. AI disruption and opportunity

## 14.1 High-value uses

AI can improve:

- tax-notice classification;
- payroll exception detection;
- benefits-question retrieval;
- HR case summarization;
- policy comparison;
- sales-call preparation;
- implementation mapping;
- claims triage;
- fraud detection;
- client-risk scoring;
- service quality review;
- regulatory-change monitoring.

Generative AI can lower the cost of routine expertise, but it can also erase differentiation if every PEO deploys the same generic assistant.

## 14.2 High-risk uses

AI should not autonomously make consequential decisions about:

- hiring;
- termination;
- promotion;
- accommodation;
- leave eligibility;
- wage deductions;
- employee classification;
- benefit eligibility;
- workers’ compensation compensability;
- legal conclusions.

Risks include hallucination, stale law, discriminatory outcomes, data leakage, privilege waiver, insufficient explanation, and overreliance by junior staff.

## 14.3 Governance

A board-approved AI program should include:

- inventory of AI systems and vendors;
- use-case risk classification;
- prohibited uses;
- approved data boundaries;
- human review;
- accuracy and bias testing;
- audit logs;
- vendor contractual controls;
- employee notice where required;
- incident response;
- periodic model reevaluation.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework provides a useful govern-map-measure-manage structure. AI should initially augment, not replace, accountable HR, payroll, legal, underwriting, and claims professionals.

# 15. 2026–2030 scenarios

These are strategic scenarios, not forecasts.

## Scenario 1: Compliance-led expansion — 30% probability

**Conditions:** Continued regulatory complexity, stable labor market, manageable insurance trend.

**Industry outcome:** 6%–9% annual WSE growth; strong demand from 10–99 employee firms; steady consolidation.

**ProService implication:** Invest in local compliance productization, referral channels, and digital onboarding.

## Scenario 2: Insurance shock — 25%

**Conditions:** Health trend, specialty drugs, hospital pricing, or workers’ compensation severity outpaces renewal pricing.

**Outcome:** Margin compression, aggressive repricing, client churn, carrier tightening, weaker providers exiting.

**Implication:** Prioritize actuarial discipline, plan design, stop-loss terms, claim analytics, and cash resilience over headline growth.

## Scenario 3: Software unbundling — 20%

**Conditions:** HCM vendors combine payroll, benefits brokerage, compliance AI, and partner-delivered employer services at materially lower price.

**Outcome:** Micro and low-complexity clients migrate; traditional providers retain regulated and complex segments but face price pressure.

**Implication:** Offer modular ASO, superior integrations, transparent outcomes, and a deliberately differentiated human-service layer.

## Scenario 4: Recession and client-credit event — 15%

**Conditions:** Tourism, construction, or small-business contraction; elevated client defaults.

**Outcome:** WSE shrinkage, lower sales, bad debt, M&A opportunity.

**Implication:** Tighten prefunding, segment exposure, prearrange liquidity, and preserve sales coverage through the downturn.

## Scenario 5: Trust premium and local winner — 10%

**Conditions:** Cyber incidents, payroll failures, and regulatory problems at weaker providers make reliability a primary buying criterion.

**Outcome:** Accredited, certified, financially transparent providers gain share despite premium pricing.

**Implication:** Market audited controls, payroll continuity, local expertise, employee experience, and quantified service quality.

# 18. The 30 metrics a ProService CEO must know cold

## Scale, mix, and growth

1. **Ending WSEs:** Total worksite employees at period end, by client size, industry, island, product, and benefit status.
2. **Average paid WSEs:** Average employees actually paid during the period; the preferred denominator for unit economics.
3. **Organic WSE growth:** Excludes acquisitions and explicitly separates new logos from retained-client employment change.
4. **New WSEs sold and activated:** Sold, implemented, and first-payroll counts should be distinct.
5. **Gross WSE retention:** Beginning WSEs retained before expansion inside remaining clients.
6. **Net WSE retention:** Retention plus expansion or contraction inside retained clients.
7. **Client-logo retention:** Clients retained, segmented by cohort and reason for loss.
8. **Industry/geographic concentration:** WSE, payroll, gross profit, and risk exposure by sector and island.
9. **Largest-client concentration:** Share of WSEs, gross profit, and unsecured payroll exposure represented by top clients.
10. **Channel concentration:** New and installed gross profit attributable to each broker, referral partner, and sales source.

## Revenue and profitability

11. **Gross payroll processed:** Employee wages administered; a measure of operational and fiduciary scale.
12. **Gross billings:** Total client invoices or debits, reconciled to payroll and pass-through obligations.
13. **Net service revenue per average WSE:** Retained administrative and service revenue divided by average WSEs.
14. **Gross profit per WSE:** Net contribution before corporate operating expenses.
15. **Health contribution per enrolled employee:** After claims or premiums, direct administration, commissions, and reserve movement.
16. **Workers’ compensation contribution per exposure unit:** After incurred losses, allocated expenses, and reserve development.
17. **Adjusted EBITDA and margin:** With every adjustment reconciled to GAAP and reviewed for recurrence.
18. **Free cash flow conversion:** Normalized free cash flow divided by normalized EBITDA, excluding client custodial cash.
19. **CAC and gross-profit payback:** Fully loaded acquisition cost divided by recurring gross profit from the acquired cohort.
20. **Cohort lifetime value:** Discounted, risk-adjusted gross profit after service, commissions, credit losses, and attrition.

## Insurance and actuarial risk

21. **Health incurred-claim trend versus pricing trend:** Overall and by plan, cohort, geography, and large-claim layer.
22. **Medical loss ratio:** Incurred claims and relevant expenses divided by premium-equivalent revenue, with reserve confidence range.
23. **Large-claim frequency and severity:** Actual versus expected, including specialty-drug exposure.
24. **Workers’ compensation loss ratio:** Accident-year and calendar-year, paid and incurred.
25. **Prior-year reserve development:** Health and workers’ compensation changes relative to prior estimates.
26. **Collateral and restricted cash:** Dollars, growth, utilization, counterparties, and headroom.

## Treasury, control, service, and resilience

27. **Payroll-at-risk before good funds:** Total and largest single-client exposure, plus approved exceptions.
28. **Tax and payroll exception inventory:** Unreconciled cash, aged tax notices, amendments, late deposits, and material payroll errors.
29. **Service quality:** First-response time, resolution time, SLA attainment, reopen rate, payroll accuracy, and client/employee satisfaction.
30. **Liquidity stress coverage:** Immediately available corporate liquidity divided by the modeled peak need under combined payroll, client-default, carrier-collateral, insurance, and cyber scenarios.

The CEO should be able to state each metric’s current value, plan, prior-year value, risk limit, trend, principal driver, accountable executive, and required corrective action. If any of these can be answered only after a special analysis, the operating system is not yet board-grade.
